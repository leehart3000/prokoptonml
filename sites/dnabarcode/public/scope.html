Scope and limits

DNAbarcode.org is cautious by design: when our data can't tell species apart, it says so instead of guessing.

What it covers

  • Mosquitoes (Culicidae): answers down to species, where our reference data and this marker can separate them. About 1,100 named mosquito species are in the reference set, out of roughly 3,700 described.
  • Other animals: a broad group only, such as an insect order. Species-level answers for other groups are planned.
  • Other markers (plant rbcL, fungal ITS, bacterial 16S) are planned, not yet available.

Kinds of answer

AnswerMeaning
A species, with % identityThe sequence is very close to references of one species, and no other species in our data is as close.
"One of: …"Several species are equally close. COI can't separate them in our data.
A species complexA known group of species (for example the Anopheles gambiae complex) that COI can't tell apart. Other markers are needed.
A genus or familyNo species in our data is close enough to name. The specimen may be a species we don't have, or a variant we haven't seen.
"Not identified"The input couldn't be used, for example because it isn't COI or is too short. The answer explains why.

How well it works

Measured on held-out mosquito sequences (details in the model card (opens in a new tab)):

  • Species in our data: wrongly named for 2.6% of species.
  • Species not in our data, with no near-identical relative in it: never named as a species (0% wrong); answered at genus level instead.
  • Species not in our data but with a near-identical relative in it: often given the relative's name (about 37% wrong). COI alone can't avoid this.
  • Other flies (not mosquitoes): none of 1,500 were taken for a mosquito.

Important limits

  • Answers describe our reference data and this marker, not the organism's scientific status. They are not an expert identification. For decisions that matter (public health, biosecurity), confirm with an expert or other markers.
  • Public sequence databases contain mislabelled records. We remove the ones we can detect, checking against NCBI's curated RefSeq genomes, but some will remain.
  • The service is a free demonstration, with limits on how often it can be used.
