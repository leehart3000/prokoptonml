# Analysis scripts

One-off analyses that informed a decision in the project. Each script is small,
numbered in the order it was written, and run in the project's locked
environment:

```bash
pixi run python analysis/<script>.py [arguments]
```

Outputs go to the git-ignored `data/` folder (or a folder you choose), never to
the repo. Anything that every pipeline run should produce belongs in a pipeline
stage instead, so it never goes stale. Scripts are linted with Ruff but are not
type-checked or covered by tests.

| Script | Question | Finding | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|
| `01_list_phyla.py` | Which phyla are in MIDORI2 GB273, and how many records does each have? | 68 named phyla (plus records with no phylum) across 15 genes (5,581,260 records); CO1 has 3,213,682 records, 5,704 without a phylum. Includes animals, fungi, plants, algae and other eukaryotes. MIDORI2's README counts are slightly higher than the files (e.g. CO1 +15, CO2 +7); our `grep -c '^>'` check matches our counts. | Build a reviewed phylum → kingdom table from NCBI lineages (`02`). Tell the MIDORI2 maintainers about the README counts after launch. |
| `02_fetch_phylum_lineages.py` | Which kingdom (or major group) does each phylum belong to? | All 68 named phyla received an NCBI lineage (fetched 2026-11-09). 31 are Metazoa (99% of CO1 records), 14 Fungi (12 of them "Fungi incertae sedis"), 3 Viridiplantae, and 20 have no kingdom rank (e.g. Sar, Rhodophyta, Amoebozoa). | Commit the reviewed result as `apps/dnabarcode/reference/phylum_lineages_gb273.tsv`. The router's top level is the group: Metazoa, Fungi, Viridiplantae or Other eukaryotes. Records with no phylum are excluded from training. |
| `03_leakage_audit.py` | Do novel-set (unseen species) sequences have near-identical matches in training, which would make novel-species scores optimistic? | In a random sample of 5,000 CO1 `test_novel` sequences (seed 273), **35.9%** (about ±1.3%) had a ≥99% identical match in `train`, 13.3% matched at 97–99%, and 50.8% matched below 97% or not at all. Of the ≥99% matches, 67% were in the same genus, 20% the same family, 10% the same order, 1.7% had an unknown order and 1.2% were in a different order. Most are placeholder-named records (e.g. "Genus sp. BIOUG…") of the held-out species; different-order matches suggest labelling errors. With thorough settings (`--min-identity 0.97 --maxaccepts 8 --maxrejects 64`), VSEARCH took about 7.6 ms per query per 250,000-sequence chunk on 8 threads, with a peak of about 1.2 GB. On the same 5,000 queries, faster settings that stop at the first ≥99% match (`--min-identity 0.99 --maxaccepts 1`) found 95.2% of the thorough run's leaks with `--maxrejects 8` (~14 s per chunk, about 3× faster) and 96.4% with `--maxrejects 32` (~23 s per chunk), with no false leaks. Neither setting is ground truth: the `--maxrejects 32` run also found 9 leaks that the thorough run missed, so about 36% of novel queries have a ≥99% training match. | Leakage is too large to ignore. Remove novel-set records with a ≥99% training match from novel-species evaluation (the targeted fix), using a reproducible pipeline stage. In the app, report near-identical matches to unidentified specimens as useful information. Estimated cost of searching all ~187,000 novel records against all training data: about 1 hour (`--maxrejects 8`), 2 hours (`--maxrejects 32`) or 4 hours (thorough). Use one global search (no per-order partitioning, so cross-order leaks are caught too), with the VSEARCH settings in the pipeline config, and document the residual leakage (roughly 1.5% of novel records). |
| `04_marker_errors.py` | Why is the marker classifier (v2) less accurate for fungi, and why does the other strand sometimes look more confident? | On up to 3,000 `test_known` records per gene and group (68,761 records): animals were 97–100% accurate for every gene, but fungi only 45–70% for CO1, Cytb, ND2, ND3, ND5 and the ribosomal RNAs (lrRNA 48%, srRNA 66%), and plants 34% for srRNA. The commonest errors were lrRNA and srRNA mistaken for each other (fungi, plants and other eukaryotes), and fungal protein-coding genes mistaken for ND2 or ND5. The other strand was more confident for 2.3% of animal, 9.2% of plant and 33.5% of fungal records, and for 23–25% of ribosomal RNA records; it was mostly predicted as a different gene (0–36% as the record's own gene), so these are model errors rather than records stored backwards. | Spread each gene's training records across groups of organisms first, then orders (`diversity = "group_order"`), and add every training record's reverse complement as a `reverse` class, so the strand is learned directly. Re-run the analysis on the new model. **Re-run on v4** (both changes): the other strand was more confident for only 0.5% of animal and 1.8% of fungal records (from 2.3% and 33.5%), but fungi stayed about 60% accurate and animals fell from 99.5% to 98.3%. **Decision:** keep the reverse class, return to spreading across orders (v5). Record the likely reason fungi stay hard (intron-rich fungal mitochondrial genes) as untested. **Re-run on v6:** animals 98.9% (CO1 99.8%), fungi 57.8%; the other strand more confident for 0.4% of animal and 4.1% of fungal records. v6 is used. |
